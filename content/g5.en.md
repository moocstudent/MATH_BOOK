## Core Notes

A single-variable derivative has only one direction to move in, but a multivariable function has several independent inputs. The central strategy of multivariable differential calculus is to *reduce many to one*: let just one variable change while the others are held fixed, giving the partial derivatives; then assemble those partials into the total differential and the gradient, and use them for the chain rule, implicit differentiation, and (constrained) extrema.

### Functions of several variables, limits and continuity

**Functions of several variables.** Given a non-empty point set $D\subset\mathbb{R}^2$, if each point $(x,y)\in D$ corresponds to a unique $z$, we obtain a function of two variables $z=f(x,y)$, and $D$ is its **domain**. For example, $z=\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}$ has the closed disk $x^2+y^2\le 1$ as its domain, while $z=\ln(x+y)$ has the half-plane $x+y>0$. The graph of a two-variable function is generally a **surface** in space.

**The double limit.** Writing $\lim_{(x,y)\to(x_0,y_0)}f(x,y)=A$ means that as the moving point $(x,y)$ approaches $(x_0,y_0)$ **in any manner, along any path**, $f$ tends to the same constant $A$. This differs from a single-variable limit, which has only a left and a right direction: in the plane there are infinitely many paths of approach, so the existence of a double limit is a far stricter demand. If two different paths give different limiting values, the double limit **does not exist**. The classic example is $f(x,y)=\dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2}$ at the origin: along $y=kx$ it tends to $\dfrac{k}{1+k^2}$, which varies with $k$, so the limit does not exist.

**Continuity.** If $\lim_{(x,y)\to(x_0,y_0)}f(x,y)=f(x_0,y_0)$, then $f$ is **continuous** at $(x_0,y_0)$. Intuitively, continuity means the surface has no "crack" or "jump" there. A multivariable elementary function is continuous throughout its domain; a function continuous on a bounded closed region must attain a maximum and a minimum there (the extreme value theorem), which underwrites the discussion of extrema below.

### Partial derivatives

**Definition.** Fix $y$ at $y_0$ and treat only $x$ as the variable, giving a single-variable function $f(x,y_0)$; its derivative at $x_0$ is the **partial derivative** of $f$ with respect to $x$:
$$f_x(x_0,y_0)=\lim_{\Delta x\to0}\frac{f(x_0+\Delta x,y_0)-f(x_0,y_0)}{\Delta x}.$$
Likewise, fixing $x$ gives the partial derivative $f_y(x_0,y_0)$ with respect to $y$. Other common notations are $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}$, $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}$, $z_x$, $z_y$. The rule of thumb is "the variable you differentiate by is the variable, everything else is a constant", so every single-variable rule carries over verbatim.

**Geometric meaning.** The plane $y=y_0$ cuts the surface $z=f(x,y)$ in a curve, and $f_x(x_0,y_0)$ is the **slope of the tangent to that trace curve** at $(x_0,y_0)$; $f_y$ is the tangent slope of the curve cut by $x=x_0$. The two partials measure how steep the surface is in the $x$-direction and the $y$-direction respectively.

**Higher-order partials.** Differentiating the partials again gives four second-order partials:
$$f_{xx}=\frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x^2},\quad f_{yy}=\frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y^2},\quad f_{xy}=\frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y\,\partial x},\quad f_{yx}=\frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x\,\partial y}.$$
Here $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ are the **mixed partials**. An important fact is that when both mixed partials are continuous, they are equal:
$$f_{xy}=f_{yx}.$$
This is **Clairaut's (Schwarz's) theorem**, and it says that in the smooth case the order of partial differentiation does not matter.

### The total differential

**Definition.** If the total increment of $z=f(x,y)$ at a point can be written
$$\Delta z=f_x\,\Delta x+f_y\,\Delta y+o(\rho),\qquad \rho=\sqrt{\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2},$$
that is, a linear part spanned by the two partials plus a quantity of higher order than $\rho$, then $f$ is **differentiable** there, and the linear leading part is the **total differential**:
$$dz=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy.$$
The total differential generalizes the single-variable differential $dy=f'(x)\,dx$: it uses a tangent plane to approximate the surface, and $dz$ is the increment along that plane.

**Several key relations.** The multivariable picture is much subtler than the single-variable one, so keep these straight:

- **Partials exist $\not\Rightarrow$ differentiable.** Unlike the single-variable case. The partials only capture change along the two coordinate directions and cannot guarantee a good linear approximation in every other direction.
- **Partials continuous $\Rightarrow$ differentiable (sufficient condition).** If $f_x$, $f_y$ exist and are continuous near a point, then $f$ is differentiable there. In practice this is the usual test for differentiability.
- **Differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous, and differentiable $\Rightarrow$ both partials exist.** Differentiability is a stronger condition than mere existence of the partials.

In short: continuous partials $\Rightarrow$ differentiable $\Rightarrow$ (continuous, and partials exist), while the reverse arrows generally fail.

**Application.** Just as with single-variable linear approximation, when $|\Delta x|,|\Delta y|$ are both small,
$$f(x_0+\Delta x,y_0+\Delta y)\approx f(x_0,y_0)+f_x\,\Delta x+f_y\,\Delta y,$$
the basic formula for estimating values and analyzing error.

### Chain rule and implicit differentiation

**The chain rule.** Let $z=f(x,y)$ where $x=x(t)$ and $y=y(t)$ are both functions of $t$, so $z$ depends on $t$ indirectly through $x,y$; its derivative with respect to $t$ is
$$\frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}.$$
It helps to draw a **dependency tree**: put $z$ at the top, branching down to $x$ and $y$, each of which connects to $t$; there are two paths from $z$ to $t$, each path multiplies the derivatives along it, and finally the paths are added. If the intermediate variables are themselves multivariable, say $x=x(s,t)$, $y=y(s,t)$, replace $\dfrac{d}{dt}$ by the partial $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}$ and the form is unchanged:
$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}.$$

**Implicit differentiation.** Let $z=z(x,y)$ be defined implicitly by $F(x,y,z)=0$. Provided $F$ has continuous partials and $F_z\ne0$, differentiate both sides with respect to $x$ (treating $z$ as a function of $x,y$) to get $F_x+F_z\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}=0$, hence
$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=-\frac{F_x}{F_z},\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-\frac{F_y}{F_z}.$$
These formulas sidestep the difficulty of "cannot solve for $z$" entirely: one only differentiates the three-variable function $F$.

### Directional derivative and gradient

**Directional derivative.** A partial derivative measures the rate of change only along a coordinate axis, whereas the **directional derivative** measures it along an arbitrary direction. For a unit vector $\hat l=(\cos\alpha,\cos\beta)$, the directional derivative of $f$ at $(x,y)$ along $\hat l$ is
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial l}=f_x\cos\alpha+f_y\cos\beta$$
(valid when $f$ is differentiable). It generalizes the partials: taking $\hat l$ along the positive $x$-axis recovers $f_x$.

**Gradient.** Assembling the two partials into one vector gives the **gradient** of $f$:
$$\nabla f=(f_x,f_y).$$
The directional derivative is then exactly the **dot product** of the gradient with the direction unit vector:
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial l}=\nabla f\cdot\hat l=|\nabla f|\cos\theta,$$
where $\theta$ is the angle between $\nabla f$ and $\hat l$. The values of $\cos\theta$ yield three conclusions: the directional derivative is largest when $\hat l$ is **aligned** with $\nabla f$ ($\theta=0$), equal to $|\nabla f|$ — so **the gradient points in the direction of fastest increase, and its magnitude is the maximum rate of change**; the opposite direction decreases fastest; and perpendicular to the gradient the function is instantaneously unchanged (along a level curve). The gradient is the cornerstone of optimization and steepest-descent algorithms.

### Extrema and constrained extrema

**Unconstrained extrema.** If $f$ has an extremum at $(x_0,y_0)$ and both partials exist there, then necessarily
$$f_x(x_0,y_0)=f_y(x_0,y_0)=0,$$
and a point satisfying this is a **critical point** (stationary point). This is only a **necessary** condition; a critical point need not be an extremum. Whether it is one is settled by the **second-derivative test**. Writing (all evaluated at the critical point)
$$A=f_{xx},\quad B=f_{xy},\quad C=f_{yy},$$
examine the discriminant $AC-B^2$:

- $AC-B^2>0$ and $A>0$: a **local minimum**;
- $AC-B^2>0$ and $A<0$: a **local maximum**;
- $AC-B^2<0$: not an extremum — the point is a **saddle point**;
- $AC-B^2=0$: the test is inconclusive and needs separate analysis.

**Constrained extrema and Lagrange multipliers.** To find the extrema of $f(x,y)$ subject to the constraint $\varphi(x,y)=0$, form the **Lagrangian**
$$L(x,y,\lambda)=f(x,y)-\lambda\,\varphi(x,y),$$
where $\lambda$ is the **Lagrange multiplier**. Setting its three partials to zero,
$$L_x=0,\quad L_y=0,\quad L_\lambda=0,$$
the last equation simply restores the constraint $\varphi=0$. Solving this system gives the candidate points for the constrained extrema. Geometrically, at an extremum the gradient of the objective is parallel to that of the constraint, i.e. $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla\varphi$.

### Summary

- A double limit requires the same value **along every path**; continuity means $\lim_{(x,y)\to(x_0,y_0)}f=f(x_0,y_0)$.
- Partial derivative $f_x=\lim_{\Delta x\to0}\frac{f(x_0+\Delta x,y_0)-f(x_0,y_0)}{\Delta x}$, "differentiate by one, hold the rest constant"; when continuous, $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$.
- Total differential $dz=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy$. **Existence of the partials does not imply differentiability; continuous partials do; differentiable implies continuous.**
- Chain rule $\frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}$; implicit $\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=-\frac{F_x}{F_z}$, $\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-\frac{F_y}{F_z}$.
- Directional derivative $\frac{\partial f}{\partial l}=\nabla f\cdot\hat l$; the gradient $\nabla f=(f_x,f_y)$ points in the direction of fastest increase, with maximum rate $|\nabla f|$.
- Necessary condition $f_x=f_y=0$; second-derivative test: with $AC-B^2>0$, $A>0$ gives a min and $A<0$ a max, while $AC-B^2<0$ gives a saddle; constrained extrema via the Lagrangian $L=f-\lambda\varphi$.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1.** Find the first-order partial derivatives of $z=x^2y+\sin(xy)$.

> **Solution.** To differentiate by $x$, treat $y$ as a constant. The first term $x^2y$ gives $2xy$; the second term $\sin(xy)$ is composite, and the inner $xy$ has partial $y$ in $x$, giving $y\cos(xy)$:
> $$z_x=2xy+y\cos(xy).$$
> To differentiate by $y$, treat $x$ as a constant. The first term gives $x^2$; the inner $xy$ has partial $x$ in $y$, giving $x\cos(xy)$:
> $$z_y=x^2+x\cos(xy).$$

**Example 2.** Find the total differential of $z=x^y$ (with $x>0$).

> **Solution.** First the two partials. Differentiating by $x$ with $y$ constant is a power function: $z_x=yx^{y-1}$. Differentiating by $y$ with $x$ constant is an exponential function: $z_y=x^y\ln x$. Hence
> $$dz=z_x\,dx+z_y\,dy=yx^{y-1}\,dx+x^y\ln x\,dy.$$

**Example 3.** Let $z=u^2v$ with $u=x+y$, $v=x-y$; find $z_x$ and $z_y$.

> **Solution.** This is a composite, so use the chain rule. First $z_u=2uv$ and $z_v=u^2$, and note $u_x=1,\ v_x=1,\ u_y=1,\ v_y=-1$. Then
> $$z_x=z_u u_x+z_v v_x=2uv+u^2=2(x+y)(x-y)+(x+y)^2=3x^2+2xy-y^2,$$
> $$z_y=z_u u_y+z_v v_y=2uv-u^2=2(x+y)(x-y)-(x+y)^2=x^2-2xy-3y^2.$$

**Example 4.** Let $z=z(x,y)$ be defined by $xyz=x+y+z$; find $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}$.

> **Solution.** Write the equation as $F(x,y,z)=xyz-x-y-z=0$. Take partials (for $F_x$, treat $y,z$ as constants):
> $$F_x=yz-1,\qquad F_z=xy-1.$$
> Substitute into the implicit-differentiation formula:
> $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=-\frac{F_x}{F_z}=-\frac{yz-1}{xy-1}=\frac{1-yz}{xy-1}.$$

**Example 5.** Find the directional derivative and gradient of $z=x^2+y^2$ at $(1,2)$ along $\vec l=(3,4)$, and the maximum rate of change.

> **Solution.** First the gradient: $\nabla z=(2x,2y)$, which at $(1,2)$ is $\nabla z=(2,4)$. The direction $\vec l=(3,4)$ has magnitude $\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5$, so the unit vector is $\hat l=\left(\tfrac35,\tfrac45\right)$. The directional derivative is the dot product of the gradient with $\hat l$:
> $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial l}=(2,4)\cdot\left(\tfrac35,\tfrac45\right)=\frac{6}{5}+\frac{16}{5}=\frac{22}{5}.$$
> The maximum rate of change is the magnitude of the gradient: $|\nabla z|=\sqrt{2^2+4^2}=\sqrt{20}=2\sqrt5$, attained in the direction $\nabla z=(2,4)$.

**Example 6.** Find the extrema of $f(x,y)=x^3+y^3-3xy$.

> **Solution.** First the critical points. Setting
> $$f_x=3x^2-3y=0,\qquad f_y=3y^2-3x=0,$$
> that is $y=x^2$ and $x=y^2$. Substituting gives $x=x^4$, i.e. $x(x^3-1)=0$, so $x=0$ or $x=1$, giving the critical points $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$. Now the second partials:
> $$A=f_{xx}=6x,\quad B=f_{xy}=-3,\quad C=f_{yy}=6y,\qquad AC-B^2=36xy-9.$$
> At $(0,0)$, $AC-B^2=-9<0$, a **saddle point**, not an extremum; at $(1,1)$, $AC-B^2=27>0$ and $A=6>0$, a **local minimum**
> $$f(1,1)=1+1-3=-1.$$

## Exercises

1. Find $z_{xx}$ and $z_{xy}$ for $z=e^{xy}$.
2. Find the total differential of $z=\ln(x^2+y^2)$.
3. Let $z=f(x^2-y^2)$ (with $f$ differentiable); show that $y\,z_x+x\,z_y=0$.
4. Let $z=z(x,y)$ be defined by $x+y+z=e^z$; find $z_x$.
5. Find the directional derivative of $u=xyz$ at $(1,2,3)$ along $\vec l=(1,1,1)$.
6. Find the extrema of $f(x,y)=x^2+y^2-xy+x-y$.

**Answers.** 1) $z_x=ye^{xy}$, so $z_{xx}=y^2e^{xy}$ and $z_{xy}=(1+xy)e^{xy}$; 2) $z_x=\dfrac{2x}{x^2+y^2}$ and $z_y=\dfrac{2y}{x^2+y^2}$, so $dz=\dfrac{2x\,dx+2y\,dy}{x^2+y^2}$; 3) by the chain rule $z_x=2xf'$ and $z_y=-2yf'$, so $y z_x+x z_y=2xyf'-2xyf'=0$; 4) let $F=x+y+z-e^z$, then $F_x=1$ and $F_z=1-e^z$, so $z_x=-\dfrac{F_x}{F_z}=\dfrac{1}{e^z-1}$; 5) $\nabla u=(yz,xz,xy)=(6,3,2)$ and $\hat l=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1,1,1)$, so $\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial l}=\dfrac{6+3+2}{\sqrt3}=\dfrac{11}{\sqrt3}=\dfrac{11\sqrt3}{3}$; 6) from $f_x=2x-y+1=0$ and $f_y=2y-x-1=0$ we get $(x,y)=\left(-\tfrac13,\tfrac13\right)$; with $A=2,B=-1,C=2$, $AC-B^2=3>0$ and $A>0$, a local minimum $f\left(-\tfrac13,\tfrac13\right)=-\tfrac13$.
