## Core Notes

The one-variable definite integral accumulates a function over a single interval of the number line. But in the real world "accumulation" often happens along curved paths and across spread-out surfaces: how heavy is a wire of uneven density? How much work does a force field do pushing a particle once around a curve? How much fluid crosses a net each second? To answer these we must extend integration from an "interval" to "curves" and "surfaces" — giving the **line integral** and the **surface integral**. Presiding over the whole chapter are three great theorems — Green's, Gauss's, and Stokes' — each of which, without exception, turns an **integral over a boundary** into an **integral over the region that boundary encloses**: the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus reincarnated in the multivariable world.

### Line integrals w.r.t. arc length

Let $L$ be a smooth curve in the plane and $f(x,y)$ a function defined on $L$. Cut $L$ into many small arcs, the $i$-th of length $\Delta s_i$, pick a point $(\xi_i,\eta_i)$ on it, form the products, sum, and take the limit: this is the **line integral of the first kind** (with respect to arc length) of $f$ along $L$,
$$\int_L f(x,y)\,ds.$$
Its physical meaning is the **mass of a wire $L$ whose linear density is $f$**: summing "density $\times$ arc length" over every small piece gives the total mass.

The way to compute it is to **parametrize**. If $L$ is given by $x=x(t),\ y=y(t)$ ($t\in[\alpha,\beta]$), the arc-length element is $ds=\sqrt{x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2}\,dt$, so the integral becomes an ordinary definite integral
$$\int_L f(x,y)\,ds=\int_\alpha^\beta f(x(t),y(t))\sqrt{x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2}\,dt.$$
Because the arc length $ds>0$ is always positive and blind to the direction of travel, the first-kind line integral is **independent of the direction of the path**: from $A$ to $B$ or from $B$ to $A$, the result is identical.

### Line integrals w.r.t. coordinates

If what we accumulate along the curve is not arc length but the **increments of the coordinates**, we obtain the **line integral of the second kind** (with respect to coordinates),
$$\int_L P(x,y)\,dx+Q(x,y)\,dy.$$
Its most typical physical setting is the **work done by a variable force along a curve**: adding up the work $P\,dx+Q\,dy$ done by the force field $(P,Q)$ over each small displacement $(dx,dy)$ gives the total work of the field along $L$.

Substituting the parametrization again, with $dx=x'(t)\,dt$ and $dy=y'(t)\,dt$, it reduces to
$$\int_L P\,dx+Q\,dy=\int_\alpha^\beta\big[P(x(t),y(t))\,x'(t)+Q(x(t),y(t))\,y'(t)\big]\,dt.$$
Unlike the first kind, the coordinate increments $dx,dy$ change sign with the direction of travel, so the second-kind line integral **depends on orientation**: reverse the orientation of $L$ and the integral flips sign.

### Green's theorem and path independence

Between the second-kind line integral in the plane and the double integral there stands a bridge: **Green's theorem**, given by the English mathematician **George Green (1828)**. Let $L$ be a piecewise-smooth **simple closed curve**, positively oriented (counter-clockwise, so the enclosed region $D$ always lies to the left of the direction of travel), and let $P,Q$ have continuous partial derivatives on $D$. Then
$$\oint_L P\,dx+Q\,dy=\iint_D\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)d\sigma.$$
A beautiful corollary computes **area from the boundary**: taking $P=-y,\ Q=x$ gives the area of $D$,
$$A=\frac12\oint_L x\,dy-y\,dx.$$

In a **simply connected** domain the following three statements are equivalent: the second-kind line integral $\int_L P\,dx+Q\,dy$ is **path-independent** (it depends only on the endpoints); the identity $\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}=\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}$ holds everywhere; and $P\,dx+Q\,dy=du$ is the **exact differential** of some function $u$ (a **potential**). In that case the integral is evaluated just like Newton–Leibniz, from the two endpoints alone:
$$\int_A^B P\,dx+Q\,dy=u(B)-u(A).$$

### Surface integrals of the first and second kind

Replace the curve by a surface and again there are two kinds of integral. The **surface integral of the first kind** (with respect to area) $\iint_\Sigma f\,dS$ is the limit of cutting $\Sigma$ into small patches, multiplying each patch's area $\Delta S$ by the value of $f$ on it, and summing; physically it is the **mass of a thin shell of surface density $f$**. If $\Sigma$ is the graph of $z=z(x,y)$ over a projection region $D_{xy}$, the area element is
$$dS=\sqrt{1+z_x^2+z_y^2}\,dx\,dy,$$
so $\iint_\Sigma f\,dS=\iint_{D_{xy}} f(x,y,z(x,y))\sqrt{1+z_x^2+z_y^2}\,dx\,dy$.

The **surface integral of the second kind** (with respect to coordinates, also called **flux**) first requires choosing a **side** of the surface — a direction fixed by the unit normal $\mathbf n$:
$$\iint_\Sigma P\,dy\,dz+Q\,dz\,dx+R\,dx\,dy=\iint_\Sigma (P,Q,R)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.$$
It measures the **flux** of the vector field $(P,Q,R)$ through the oriented surface. Reverse the side and the integral changes sign — echoing the orientation-dependence of the second-kind line integral.

### The Gauss (divergence) theorem

The flux through a closed surface can be turned into a triple integral over the solid it bounds: this is the **Gauss theorem**, also called the **divergence theorem** — established by **C. F. Gauss (1813)** and **M. Ostrogradsky (who gave the general proof in 1826)**. Let $\Sigma$ be a piecewise-smooth **closed surface**, oriented **outward**, bounding a solid $\Omega$. Then
$$\oiint_\Sigma P\,dy\,dz+Q\,dz\,dx+R\,dx\,dy=\iiint_\Omega\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial Q}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial R}{\partial z}\right)dV.$$
The quantity in parentheses is the **divergence** $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F$ of the field. The theorem says: the **total flux out** of a closed surface equals the accumulation of the field's divergence throughout its interior — the divergence measures the strength of the "source" at each point, and the divergence theorem sums those pointwise sources into the total outflow.

### Stokes' theorem

The spatial generalization of Green's theorem is **Stokes' theorem**, and its name carries a curious history: the formula was first **proposed by Lord Kelvin (W. Thomson, 1850)**, while **G. G. Stokes set it as a Cambridge examination question in 1854**, after which his name stuck. Let $\Sigma$ be an oriented surface whose **boundary curve** $\Gamma$ is oriented in accordance with the side of $\Sigma$ by the right-hand rule. Then
$$\oint_\Gamma P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz=\iint_\Sigma\left(\frac{\partial R}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial Q}{\partial z}\right)dy\,dz+\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial R}{\partial x}\right)dz\,dx+\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)dx\,dy=\iint_\Sigma(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.$$
The right-hand side is exactly the flux of the field's **curl** $\nabla\times\mathbf F$, so the theorem says: the **circulation** around the boundary $\Gamma$ equals the flux of the curl through the surface. When $\Sigma$ degenerates to a flat region and the field lies in the plane, the curl keeps only the single term $\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}$, and Stokes' theorem reduces to **Green's theorem** — Green is merely the planar special case of Stokes.

### Summary

- First-kind line integral $\int_L f\,ds$ (arc length, the mass of a wire): parametrize with $ds=\sqrt{x'^2+y'^2}\,dt$; **independent of direction**.
- Second-kind line integral $\int_L P\,dx+Q\,dy$ (coordinates, work of a variable force): compute via $dx=x'\,dt,\ dy=y'\,dt$; **flips sign under reversal**.
- Green's theorem $\oint_L P\,dx+Q\,dy=\iint_D(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y})\,d\sigma$; area $A=\frac12\oint_L x\,dy-y\,dx$. In a simply connected domain, path-independence $\iff\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\iff$ a potential $u$ exists, with $\int_A^B=u(B)-u(A)$.
- First-kind surface integral $\iint_\Sigma f\,dS$ uses $dS=\sqrt{1+z_x^2+z_y^2}\,dx\,dy$; the second kind (flux) $\iint_\Sigma(P,Q,R)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS$ **flips sign with the side**.
- Gauss: $\oiint_\Sigma(P,Q,R)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=\iiint_\Omega(\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial Q}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial R}{\partial z})\,dV$, total outflow $=$ accumulated divergence.
- Stokes: $\oint_\Gamma P\,dx+Q\,dy+R\,dz=\iint_\Sigma(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS$, circulation $=$ flux of the curl; Green is its planar special case.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1.** Find $\displaystyle\int_L (x+y)\,ds$, where $L$ is the straight segment from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$.

> **Solution.** Parametrize the segment as $x=t,\ y=t$ ($t\in[0,1]$), so $x'=y'=1$ and $ds=\sqrt{1^2+1^2}\,dt=\sqrt2\,dt$. The integrand $x+y=2t$, hence
> $$\int_L (x+y)\,ds=\int_0^1 2t\cdot\sqrt2\,dt=2\sqrt2\left[\frac{t^2}{2}\right]_0^1=\sqrt2.$$

**Example 2.** Find $\displaystyle\int_L xy\,dx$, where $L$ is the arc of the parabola $y=x^2$ from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$.

> **Solution.** This is a second-kind line integral. Take $x=t,\ y=t^2$ ($t\in[0,1]$), so $dx=dt$ and $xy=t\cdot t^2=t^3$; therefore
> $$\int_L xy\,dx=\int_0^1 t^3\,dt=\left[\frac{t^4}{4}\right]_0^1=\frac14.$$

**Example 3.** Use Green's theorem to find $\displaystyle\oint_L (x^2-y)\,dx+(x+y^2)\,dy$, where $L$ is the unit circle $x^2+y^2=1$, positively oriented.

> **Solution.** Here $P=x^2-y$ and $Q=x+y^2$, so $\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}=1-(-1)=2$. Green's theorem turns the integral into a double integral over the unit disk $D$:
> $$\oint_L (x^2-y)\,dx+(x+y^2)\,dy=\iint_D 2\,d\sigma=2\cdot(\pi\cdot1^2)=2\pi.$$

**Example 4.** Verify that $\displaystyle\int_L 2xy\,dx+x^2\,dy$ is path-independent, and evaluate it from $(0,0)$ to $(1,2)$.

> **Solution.** Here $P=2xy$ and $Q=x^2$, and $\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}=2x=\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}$, so on the whole (simply connected) plane the integral is **path-independent**. Find a potential $u$ with $du=2xy\,dx+x^2\,dy$: from $u_x=2xy$ we get $u=x^2y+g(y)$, and then $u_y=x^2+g'(y)=x^2$ forces $g'(y)=0$, so take $u=x^2y$. Hence
> $$\int_{(0,0)}^{(1,2)} 2xy\,dx+x^2\,dy=u(1,2)-u(0,0)=1^2\cdot2-0=2.$$

**Example 5.** Find $\displaystyle\iint_\Sigma z\,dS$, where $\Sigma$ is the part of the plane $z=x+y$ lying over $D=[0,1]\times[0,1]$.

> **Solution.** From $z=x+y$ we get $z_x=z_y=1$, so $dS=\sqrt{1+1^2+1^2}\,dx\,dy=\sqrt3\,dx\,dy$. On $\Sigma$ we have $z=x+y$, hence
> $$\iint_\Sigma z\,dS=\iint_D (x+y)\sqrt3\,dx\,dy=\sqrt3\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 (x+y)\,dx\,dy=\sqrt3\cdot 1=\sqrt3.$$
> Here the inner integral is $\int_0^1\!\int_0^1(x+y)\,dx\,dy=\int_0^1\left(\frac12+y\right)dy=1$.

**Example 6.** Use the Gauss theorem to find $\displaystyle\oiint_\Sigma x\,dy\,dz+y\,dz\,dx+z\,dx\,dy$, where $\Sigma$ is the unit sphere $x^2+y^2+z^2=1$, oriented outward.

> **Solution.** Here $P=x,\ Q=y,\ R=z$, and the divergence is $\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial x}+\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial y}+\dfrac{\partial R}{\partial z}=1+1+1=3$. Gauss's theorem turns it into a triple integral over the unit ball $\Omega$:
> $$\oiint_\Sigma x\,dy\,dz+y\,dz\,dx+z\,dx\,dy=\iiint_\Omega 3\,dV=3\cdot\frac{4}{3}\pi\cdot1^3=4\pi.$$

## Exercises

1. Find $\displaystyle\int_L y\,ds$, where $L$ is the upper half of the unit circle, from $(1,0)$ to $(-1,0)$.
2. Find $\displaystyle\int_L x\,dx+y\,dy$, where $L$ is the straight segment from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$.
3. Use Green's theorem to find $\displaystyle\oint_L -y\,dx+x\,dy$, where $L$ is the positively oriented unit circle.
4. Verify that $\displaystyle\int_L (2x+y)\,dx+(x+2y)\,dy$ is path-independent and find a potential function.
5. Find $\displaystyle\iint_\Sigma dS$, where $\Sigma$ is the part of the plane $z=2x+2y+1$ lying over $D=[0,1]\times[0,1]$.
6. Use the Gauss theorem to find $\displaystyle\oiint_\Sigma x\,dy\,dz+y\,dz\,dx+z\,dx\,dy$, where $\Sigma$ is the surface of the cube $[0,1]^3$, oriented outward.

**Answers.** 1) take $x=\cos\theta,\ y=\sin\theta$ ($\theta\in[0,\pi]$), so $ds=\sqrt{\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta}\,d\theta=d\theta$ and $\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta=[-\cos\theta]_0^\pi=2$; 2) take $x=t,\ y=t$, giving $\int_0^1(t+t)\,dt=\int_0^1 2t\,dt=1$; 3) $P=-y,\ Q=x$, so $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=1-(-1)=2$ and $\iint_D 2\,d\sigma=2\pi$ (exactly twice the "area from the boundary" formula); 4) $\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=1=\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}$, so it is path-independent, with potential $u=x^2+xy+y^2$ (check $u_x=2x+y,\ u_y=x+2y$); 5) $z_x=z_y=2$, so $dS=\sqrt{1+2^2+2^2}\,dx\,dy=3\,dx\,dy$ and $\iint_D 3\,dx\,dy=3$; 6) the divergence is $1+1+1=3$, so $\iiint_\Omega 3\,dV=3\cdot1=3$ (the cube has volume $1$).
