## Core Notes

A definite integral is the number you get by accumulating a function's values over an interval and passing to a limit. Move the stage of accumulation from a one-dimensional interval to a planar region and you get the double integral; move it to a solid in space and you get the triple integral. Together they are the **multiple integrals**. Although their definitions are two- and three-dimensional limits of sums, the actual computation almost always reduces to single-variable definite integrals we already know — iterated integration peels the "many dimensions" into one dimension at a time. Choosing the right order, or switching to polar coordinates, is often what decides whether the calculation can be carried out at all.

### Concept and properties of the double integral

**Definition.** Let $f(x,y)$ be defined on a bounded closed region $D$. Partition $D$ arbitrarily into $n$ small pieces, write $\Delta\sigma_i$ for the area of the $i$-th piece, pick a point $(\xi_i,\eta_i)$ in it, and form the sum $\sum_{i=1}^n f(\xi_i,\eta_i)\Delta\sigma_i$. If this sum tends to a limit as the largest diameter $\lambda$ of the pieces goes to $0$, then $f$ is **integrable** on $D$, and the limit is the **double integral** of $f$ over $D$:
$$\iint_D f(x,y)\,d\sigma = \lim_{\lambda\to0}\sum_{i=1}^n f(\xi_i,\eta_i)\Delta\sigma_i,$$
where $d\sigma$ is the **area element**. Every function continuous on $D$ is integrable.

**Geometric meaning.** When $f(x,y)\ge0$, $\iint_D f\,d\sigma$ is exactly the volume of the **solid** standing on $D$ as its base and capped by the surface $z=f(x,y)$; in general it is a **signed volume** (the part of the surface below the $xy$-plane counts as negative). This runs perfectly parallel to the definite integral as "signed area" — only one dimension higher.

**Properties.** The double integral inherits all the basic properties of the definite integral:

- Linearity: $\iint_D [af+bg]\,d\sigma = a\iint_D f\,d\sigma + b\iint_D g\,d\sigma$;
- Additivity over regions: if $D=D_1\cup D_2$ with no common interior points, then $\iint_D = \iint_{D_1}+\iint_{D_2}$;
- Sign preservation and comparison: $f\le g$ implies $\iint_D f\,d\sigma\le\iint_D g\,d\sigma$;
- Mean-value theorem: if $f$ is continuous on $D$ and $D$ has area $\sigma$, then there is a point $(\xi,\eta)\in D$ with $\iint_D f\,d\sigma = f(\xi,\eta)\,\sigma$.

In particular, taking $f\equiv1$ gives $\iint_D 1\,d\sigma = \sigma$, the area of the region $D$.

### Rectangular iterated integrals

The two-dimensional sum in the definition cannot be computed directly; the workable method is to turn it into **two successive single integrals** (an iterated integral). Everything hinges on the shape of the region.

If $D$ is an **X-type region** — one that can be written as
$$D:\ a\le x\le b,\quad y_1(x)\le y\le y_2(x),$$
with continuous lower and upper boundaries $y_1,y_2$ of $x$ — then
$$\iint_D f(x,y)\,d\sigma = \int_a^b\left[\int_{y_1(x)}^{y_2(x)} f(x,y)\,dy\right]dx = \int_a^b dx\int_{y_1(x)}^{y_2(x)} f(x,y)\,dy.$$
Read it thus: fix $x$ first, integrate $f$ as a function of $y$ over $[y_1(x),y_2(x)]$ (obtaining a function of $x$ alone — geometrically the area of the cross-section at $x$), then integrate over $x$ from $a$ to $b$ (accumulating the slice volumes along $x$).

If $D$ is a **Y-type region** $c\le y\le d,\ x_1(y)\le x\le x_2(y)$, then **integrate over $x$ first**:
$$\iint_D f(x,y)\,d\sigma = \int_c^d dy\int_{x_1(y)}^{x_2(y)} f(x,y)\,dx.$$

The statement "fix one variable and split the double integral into two layers of single integrals" is **Fubini's theorem**. For a continuous function on a rectangle $[a,b]\times[c,d]$ both orders are available and give the same result; if moreover the integrand separates as $f(x,y)=g(x)h(y)$, the double integral factors into a product of single integrals: $\iint_D g(x)h(y)\,d\sigma = \big(\int_a^b g\,dx\big)\big(\int_c^d h\,dy\big)$. Sketching the region and reading off the inner and outer limits is the first step of such a computation — and the easiest step to get wrong.

### Swapping the order of integration

For one and the same double integral the X-type and Y-type iterated forms are numerically equal, yet they can differ **enormously in difficulty**: sometimes integrating over $y$ first leads to no elementary antiderivative at all, while the other order resolves at once. This gives a basic skill — **swapping the order of integration**.

The method has three steps:

1. **Read the limits, draw the region.** From the given iterated integral (say $\int_a^b dx\int_{y_1(x)}^{y_2(x)}f\,dy$) reconstruct the region $D$ and sketch it; the inner limits are functions of $x$ — draw those two curves — and the outer constant limits give the range of $x$.
2. **Re-describe from the other direction.** For the same $D$, slice it with the other family of lines: set the range of $y$ as constant limits $c\le y\le d$, then express the left and right boundaries as $x=x_1(y)$, $x=x_2(y)$.
3. **Write the new order.** This yields $\int_c^d dy\int_{x_1(y)}^{x_2(y)} f\,dx$.

The point throughout: **only the description of the region changes; the region $D$ itself does not**. So the sketch is the lifeline, and the boundary curves must be solved for the other variable after the swap. Integrands with no elementary antiderivative, such as $\int e^{y^2}\,dy$ or $\int\frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx$, are exactly the ones that swapping the order often renders integrable.

### The double integral in polar coordinates

When the region is a disk, an annulus, or a sector, or the integrand involves $x^2+y^2$, rectangular coordinates tend to give messy limits and hard integrals, whereas **polar coordinates** clear the fog. Under the substitution
$$x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,$$
the crucial change is in the area element. Partitioning the plane by concentric circles ($r$ constant) and rays ($\theta$ constant), a small cell is approximately a rectangle with sides $dr$ and $r\,d\theta$, so
$$d\sigma = r\,dr\,d\theta.$$
That extra factor $r$ (the Jacobian) **must not be dropped**. Hence
$$\iint_D f(x,y)\,d\sigma = \iint_D f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta,$$
which is then turned into an iterated integral (usually $r$ first, then $\theta$). For instance the whole disk $x^2+y^2\le a^2$ is described by $0\le\theta\le2\pi,\ 0\le r\le a$; the annulus $1\le x^2+y^2\le4$ by $0\le\theta\le2\pi,\ 1\le r\le2$ — the limits all become constants, clean and tidy. The test is simple: **a region with circular boundaries, or an integrand that is a function of $x^2+y^2$**, calls for polar coordinates first.

### A first look at triple integrals

Raise the stage of accumulation one more dimension and you reach the **triple integral**. Let $f(x,y,z)$ be defined on a bounded closed region $\Omega$ in space; partitioning, summing, and taking the limit just as for the double integral gives
$$\iiint_\Omega f(x,y,z)\,dV = \lim_{\lambda\to0}\sum_i f(\xi_i,\eta_i,\zeta_i)\Delta V_i,$$
where $dV$ is the **volume element**, equal to $dx\,dy\,dz$ in rectangular coordinates. Taking $f\equiv1$ makes $\iiint_\Omega 1\,dV$ the volume of $\Omega$.

Computation again proceeds by reduction to iterated integrals, and there are two common ways to slice:

- **One then two (project and pierce).** Project $\Omega$ onto the $xy$-plane to get a region $D$; over each point $(x,y)$ of $D$, $z$ runs from a lower surface $z_1(x,y)$ up to an upper surface $z_2(x,y)$. Then $\iiint_\Omega f\,dV = \iint_D\Big[\int_{z_1(x,y)}^{z_2(x,y)} f\,dz\Big]d\sigma$ — a single integral along one vertical line first, then a double integral over $D$.
- **Two then one (slice in order).** Cut $\Omega$ into thin slabs along the $z$-axis; the cross-section at a fixed $z$ is $D_z$, and $\iiint_\Omega f\,dV = \int_{c}^{d}\Big[\iint_{D_z} f\,d\sigma\Big]dz$ — especially handy when the cross-sectional area is easy and the integrand depends only on $z$.

If $\Omega$ has rotational or spherical symmetry, switching to **cylindrical coordinates** ($dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$) or **spherical coordinates** ($dV=r^2\sin\varphi\,dr\,d\varphi\,d\theta$) saves effort, for exactly the same reason that polar coordinates help in the plane.

### Applications of multiple integrals

Multiple integrals apply wherever a quantity is "the total of something distributed over a region". A few basic models:

**Planar area and volume.** The area of a region $D$ is $A=\iint_D 1\,d\sigma$; the volume of the solid on base $D$ capped by $z=f(x,y)\ge0$ is $V=\iint_D f(x,y)\,d\sigma$. The volume between two surfaces $z=f$ (above) and $z=g$ (below) is $\iint_D [f-g]\,d\sigma$.

**Mass.** If a thin plate occupies a region $D$ with area density $\rho(x,y)$, its total mass is
$$M=\iint_D \rho(x,y)\,d\sigma.$$
This is the physical prototype of the double integral: cut the plate into small pieces, each of mass about $\rho\,\Delta\sigma$, then sum and pass to the limit.

**Centroid (center of mass).** The center of mass of that plate has coordinates
$$\bar x=\frac{1}{M}\iint_D x\,\rho\,d\sigma,\qquad \bar y=\frac{1}{M}\iint_D y\,\rho\,d\sigma.$$
The numerators $\iint_D x\rho\,d\sigma$ and $\iint_D y\rho\,d\sigma$ are the plate's **first moments** about the $y$-axis and the $x$-axis. When the density is uniform ($\rho$ constant) it cancels, and the center of mass depends only on the shape: $\bar x=\frac1A\iint_D x\,d\sigma$, $\bar y=\frac1A\iint_D y\,d\sigma$ — then called the **geometric centroid**. The same idea carries into space, where a triple integral gives the mass and center of mass of a solid.

### Summary

- The double integral $\iint_D f\,d\sigma=\lim_{\lambda\to0}\sum_i f(\xi_i,\eta_i)\Delta\sigma_i$ is a limit of sums over a region, geometrically the signed volume under $z=f(x,y)$; continuous ⇒ integrable; $\iint_D 1\,d\sigma$ is the area.
- Reduce to iterated integrals in rectangular coordinates (Fubini): X-type $\int_a^b dx\int_{y_1(x)}^{y_2(x)}f\,dy$; Y-type integrates over $x$ first. Sketching the region and fixing the limits comes first.
- Swapping the order: reconstruct and sketch $D$ from the iterated integral, then re-read its limits with the other family of lines; the region is unchanged, only its description. Use it when one order will not integrate.
- Polar coordinates: $x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta$, $d\sigma=r\,dr\,d\theta$ (never drop the factor $r$). First choice for disks, annuli, or integrands in $x^2+y^2$.
- The triple integral $\iiint_\Omega f\,dV$ is computed by "one then two" projecting-and-piercing or "two then one" slicing; use cylindrical or spherical coordinates when symmetric.
- Applications: area $\iint_D 1\,d\sigma$, volume $\iint_D f\,d\sigma$, mass $\iint_D\rho\,d\sigma$, centroid $\bar x=\frac1M\iint_D x\rho\,d\sigma$ (the density cancels when uniform, giving the geometric centroid).

## Worked Examples

**Example 1.** Find $\displaystyle\iint_D xy\,d\sigma$, where $D:\ 0\le x\le1,\ 0\le y\le2$.

> **Solution.** $D$ is a rectangle and the integrand separates as $x\cdot y$, so the double integral factors into a product of single integrals:
> $$\iint_D xy\,d\sigma = \int_0^1 x\,dx\int_0^2 y\,dy = \left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^1\left[\frac{y^2}{2}\right]_0^2 = \frac12\cdot2 = 1.$$

**Example 2.** Find $\displaystyle\iint_D x\,d\sigma$, where $D$ is bounded by $y=x$ and $y=x^2$.

> **Solution.** The two curves meet at $x=0$ and $x=1$; on $[0,1]$ we have $x^2\le x$, so $D$ is the X-type region $0\le x\le1,\ x^2\le y\le x$. Integrate over $y$ first:
> $$\iint_D x\,d\sigma = \int_0^1 x\,dx\int_{x^2}^{x} dy = \int_0^1 x\,(x-x^2)\,dx = \int_0^1 (x^2-x^3)\,dx = \frac13-\frac14 = \frac1{12}.$$

**Example 3.** Swap the order of integration to evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1 dx\int_x^1 e^{y^2}\,dy$.

> **Solution.** Since $e^{y^2}$ has no elementary antiderivative in $y$, we must swap. The region of the original integral is $0\le x\le1,\ x\le y\le1$, i.e. the triangle $\{x\le y\}$. Describe it as Y-type: $0\le y\le1,\ 0\le x\le y$. Then
> $$\int_0^1 dx\int_x^1 e^{y^2}\,dy = \int_0^1 dy\int_0^{y} e^{y^2}\,dx = \int_0^1 e^{y^2}\cdot y\,dy.$$
> Let $u=y^2$, $du=2y\,dy$:
> $$= \frac12\int_0^1 e^{u}\,du = \frac12(e-1).$$

**Example 4.** Use polar coordinates to find $\displaystyle\iint_D e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,d\sigma$, where $D:\ x^2+y^2\le a^2$.

> **Solution.** The region is a disk and the integrand involves $x^2+y^2$ — a perfect fit for polar coordinates. Describe $D$ as $0\le\theta\le2\pi,\ 0\le r\le a$, with $x^2+y^2=r^2$ and $d\sigma=r\,dr\,d\theta$:
> $$\iint_D e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,d\sigma = \int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\int_0^a e^{-r^2}\,r\,dr = 2\pi\left[-\frac12 e^{-r^2}\right]_0^a = 2\pi\cdot\frac12\left(1-e^{-a^2}\right) = \pi\left(1-e^{-a^2}\right).$$

**Example 5.** Use polar coordinates to find $\displaystyle\iint_D \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\,d\sigma$, where $D:\ x^2+y^2\le1$.

> **Solution.** Again pass to polar coordinates, with $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=r$ and $D:\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi,\ 0\le r\le1$:
> $$\iint_D \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\,d\sigma = \int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\int_0^1 r\cdot r\,dr = 2\pi\int_0^1 r^2\,dr = 2\pi\cdot\frac13 = \frac{2\pi}{3}.$$

**Example 6.** Find the volume of the solid bounded by the surface $z=4-x^2-y^2$ and the plane $z=0$.

> **Solution.** The solid is capped by the paraboloid and sits on the $xy$-plane; the cap meets $z=0$ where $4-x^2-y^2=0$, the circle $x^2+y^2=4$. So the volume is that of the solid under the surface:
> $$V = \iint_{x^2+y^2\le4}(4-x^2-y^2)\,d\sigma.$$
> In polar coordinates, $D:\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi,\ 0\le r\le2$ and $4-x^2-y^2=4-r^2$:
> $$V = \int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\int_0^2 (4-r^2)\,r\,dr = 2\pi\left[2r^2-\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2 = 2\pi\,(8-4) = 8\pi.$$

## Exercises

1. Find $\displaystyle\iint_D (x+y)\,d\sigma$, where $D:[0,1]\times[0,1]$.
2. Find $\displaystyle\iint_D xy\,d\sigma$, where $D$ is bounded by $y=x^2$, $y=1$, $x=0$ (first quadrant).
3. Swap the order of integration in $\displaystyle\int_0^1 dy\int_y^1 f(x,y)\,dx$.
4. Use polar coordinates to find $\displaystyle\iint_D (x^2+y^2)\,d\sigma$, where $D:\ x^2+y^2\le4$.
5. Use a double integral to find the area of the annulus $D:\ 1\le x^2+y^2\le4$.
6. Find the volume of the solid under the surface $z=xy$ over $D:[0,1]\times[0,1]$.

**Answers.** 1) $\int_0^1 dx\int_0^1 (x+y)\,dy = \int_0^1 (x+\frac12)\,dx = \frac12+\frac12 = 1$; 2) the region is $0\le x\le1,\ x^2\le y\le1$, so $\int_0^1 x\,dx\int_{x^2}^1 y\,dy = \frac12\int_0^1 x(1-x^4)\,dx = \frac12\int_0^1 (x-x^5)\,dx = \frac12(\frac12-\frac16) = \frac16$; 3) the region $0\le y\le1,\ y\le x\le1$ becomes $0\le x\le1,\ 0\le y\le x$, i.e. $\int_0^1 dx\int_0^x f(x,y)\,dy$; 4) in polar $D:0\le\theta\le2\pi,\ 0\le r\le2$, giving $\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\int_0^2 r^2\cdot r\,dr = 2\pi\big[\frac{r^4}{4}\big]_0^2 = 2\pi\cdot4 = 8\pi$; 5) $\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\int_1^2 r\,dr = 2\pi\big[\frac{r^2}{2}\big]_1^2 = 2\pi\cdot\frac32 = 3\pi$; 6) $\iint_D xy\,d\sigma = \int_0^1 x\,dx\int_0^1 y\,dy = \frac12\cdot\frac12 = \frac14$.
